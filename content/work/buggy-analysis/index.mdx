The model was a downloaded off-road buggy: a tube-frame roll cage with seats, suspension and tires. The team ran four studies on it, and I ran two: drag and a frontal collision.

## Drag

The CAD was built from surfaces that wouldn't turn into solids, and Fluent needs a solid to subtract from the air. I swapped in an equivalent solid-body frame, set it in an enclosure, and ran air at 45 mph through a steady k-epsilon model. The drag coefficient came out 0.544 in the run we presented and 0.5376 in a longer run for the written report, well above the 0.25 to 0.3 we'd used as the benchmark for a modern car. Neither run had fully converged, and the feedback asked for tighter convergence and symmetry walls.

## Collision, and what it taught me

The same surface problem hit the collision study. To get the frame to mesh, I gave all 31 bodies a 60 mm shell. That turned a tube frame into about 9 t of steel, and because the impact force came from that mass stopping from 40 mph in 0.01 s, the load was just as inflated. The student license capped the mesh at 32,000 nodes, so the elements also had to be about 1,000 times coarser than the ANSYS default.

Deformation came out at 143 mm against an 1,800 mm allowance, which read as a pass. Peak stress didn't: about 6.7 GPa, roughly 27 times the steel's 250 MPa yield. That number said more about my model than about the frame, so I went back and refined the model.
