## The problem

Wind and solar don't produce on demand, so the grid needs somewhere to park the surplus. Terrament's answer is gravity: heavy modules ride a rail bolted to the wall of a deep mine shaft, climbing on motors when there's power to spare and generating on the way back down as those same motors brake them. The company pitches it as pumped hydro without the hill or the water. The concept, the patents and every performance figure on its site are Terrament's.

My part was the hardware, at 1/24 scale, and then the CAD for the mechanism that replaced it. The three models below are the three generations in the order I worked on them.

## First: the geared module

The module in Terrament's patent (US 12,272,947) climbs a rack on three geared wheels, with motors in the outer two and a cycloidal reduction in the one in the middle. I modeled the motor assembly, the gear drive and the bearings in Fusion 360, then assembled the prototype from printed gears and racks, off-the-shelf pillow-block bearings and an aluminum extrusion tower. I wired the motors and wrote the Arduino code that runs them.

It was built for Newlab's New Climate Futures event in September 2023. The final assembly file is saved as "(9/20)", the day before the event, and the part names in it still carry the rework: a corrected shaft hub, a second axle cap, a new double bearing, new top and bottom bases.

Around both designs I also worked on the numbers: load and gear-ratio calculations, structural feasibility checks in Fusion 360, and FEA for stress distribution and fatigue life that fed the choice between metal and plastic parts.

## Why it changed

The geared module worked at 1/24 scale. But the real thing has to go down a mine shaft, and the redesign was meant to move the weight more efficiently and suit a full-size shaft installation better. It traded the rotating geared wheels for a linear harmonic drive: the module carries pins or rollers that step along a profiled rail.

## Second: six pins in a slot

The first linear version was a carriage with six pins running in a zigzag slot. I sketched it in SketchUp, rebuilt it in Fusion 360, and calculated the pin gap, pin sizes and cycling ratios along the way.

<Model name="pin-concept" caption="Generation 2: the first linear harmonic concept, a six-pin carriage in a profiled slot." />

## Third: the linear harmonic drive

The last version moves the drive into the module. A motor turns a belt on two drive wheels, the belt carries a column of rollers that engage a cam-profiled I-beam rail, and ballast castings supply the weight. I 3D-printed a small version as a proof of concept.

The file I have is named "Assembly_Old_7/11/24". So by the middle of July that layout had already been replaced, two months before my internship ended.
